## Script

### 0:00 Cold open: Worth Less Than a Slice

In 2025, the Chinese giant Tencent paid **one point one six billion euros** for a minority
stake in a new company that holds just three of Ubisoft's series: Assassin's Creed, Far Cry
and Rainbow Six.
`[VISUAL: a stack of euro bills slides across a table toward three game boxes: AC, Far Cry, R6]`

Today, the **entire** company, every studio and every game, is worth about **six hundred
and eighty million euros.**
`[VISUAL: a big number counter spins down: €1.16B … then €680M stamped in red]`

Back in 2018, one Ubisoft share cost over **a hundred euros** **[CHECK: peak ≈ €107, July 2018]**.
This week it closed around **four**.
`[VISUAL: a stock chart; a green line climbs to a peak, then a long red slide to €4.08]`

Six games cancelled. Studios closed. A record loss. And a hacker who handed out millions of
dollars' worth of free currency inside their biggest online game.
`[VISUAL: quick cuts: "CANCELLED" stamps, a locked studio door, a hacker silhouette, coins raining]`

So how does the company that made Assassin's Creed end up worth less than what someone paid
for a slice of just three of its games? To answer that, you have to see how high Ubisoft
climbed. The way it climbed is exactly how it fell.
`[VISUAL: title card: UBISOFT, with a crack running through the logo]`

### 1:05 Five Brothers From Brittany

1986. A small town in Brittany, in the northwest of France. Five brothers named Guillemot,
whose family sold farm supplies, start a business selling software by mail order
**[CHECK: founding details, Carentoir]**.
`[VISUAL: AI-painted French countryside, a farmhouse, five brothers around a kitchen table with floppy disks]`

They don't stay a distributor for long. In 1995 they release *Rayman*, a cartoon hero with
no arms and no legs, and it sells millions **[CHECK: Rayman sales]**.
`[VISUAL: a limbless cartoon character bouncing across a 2D level]`

Then they start collecting worlds. They buy Red Storm, the studio behind the Tom Clancy
games, and suddenly Ubisoft owns *Rainbow Six*, *Ghost Recon* and, in 2002, *Splinter Cell*
**[CHECK: Red Storm acquisition 2000]**. In 2003 comes *Prince of Persia: The Sands of Time*,
a game people still call a masterpiece.
`[VISUAL: a shelf filling up with game boxes, one by one, each landing with a thud]`

And in 2007, a studio in Montreal releases a game about a hooded assassin climbing real
historical cities. *Assassin's Creed* becomes one of the biggest new series of its
generation **[CHECK: first-game sales figure]**.
`[VISUAL: a hooded figure on a rooftop over medieval Jerusalem; the camera pulls back to show the whole city]`

Then Ubisoft finds a second engine. *Far Cry* turns into a blockbuster series. *Just Dance*,
a game where you just copy the moves on screen, sells to families who have never bought a
"real" video game **[CHECK: Just Dance series sales]**. Ubisoft's studios spread across the
world: Montreal, Toronto, Shanghai, Kyiv, Pune. Tens of thousands of people build Ubisoft
games **[CHECK: peak headcount ≈ 20,000]**.
`[VISUAL: a world map lighting up with studio pins, one after another]`

Ubisoft is now a giant. And then the giant gets hunted.

In 2015, the French billionaire Vincent Bolloré, through his media group Vivendi, starts
quietly buying Ubisoft shares. The brothers recognize the playbook: Vivendi had just taken
over another game company, Gameloft, the same way **[CHECK: Gameloft takeover 2016]**.
`[VISUAL: a shadowy figure in a suit placing chess pieces on a board shaped like Ubisoft's logo]`

Vivendi keeps buying, until it owns more than a quarter of Ubisoft **[CHECK: Vivendi stake ≈ 27%]**.
The brothers fly around the world asking investors to stand with them. They open studios,
announce games and push the share price up, because the higher the price, the more expensive
a takeover becomes.
`[VISUAL: the brothers pushing a stock chart upward like a boulder up a hill]`

The Guillemots fight for three years. In 2018 they win. Vivendi sells, Tencent buys a small
stake as a friendly shield, and the family keeps control **[CHECK: 2018 Vivendi exit and Tencent ~5% stake]**.
That same year, Ubisoft's share price hits its all-time high.
`[VISUAL: the five brothers' silhouettes standing on top of the stock chart's peak]`

They had beaten a billionaire. They had built worlds millions of people loved. And they had
found something even more valuable: a formula.

### 3:30 The Formula

Here's how it worked. Build a huge open world. Cover the map in icons: towers to climb,
outposts to capture, collectibles to find. Climb a tower and the map reveals more icons.
`[VISUAL: a fantasy map filling with hundreds of icons, popping in faster and faster]`

It worked in Assassin's Creed. It worked in Far Cry. It worked in Watch Dogs. Players even
gave it a name: **the Ubisoft formula**.
`[VISUAL: three different game maps side by side, all with the same tower icon in the middle]`

And for a company that has to ship games on schedule to keep shareholders happy, a formula
is a dream. You don't have to invent something new every time. You build a bigger map and
add more icons.
`[VISUAL: an assembly line stamping out game boxes, each one bigger than the last]`

But a formula only works as long as the people buying it haven't noticed it's a formula.

### 4:20 The Cracks

The first cracks showed up in 2014.

At a trade show in 2012, Ubisoft had shown *Watch Dogs*: rain-soaked streets, realistic
lighting, a game that looked a generation ahead. When it launched in 2014, it looked
noticeably worse, and players accused Ubisoft of a "downgrade" **[CHECK: E3 2012 reveal vs 2014 launch]**.
`[VISUAL: a split screen: glossy, rainy city on the left, flat grey version on the right]`

A few months later came *Assassin's Creed Unity*. It launched so broken that characters
walked around with no faces, just floating eyes and teeth. The screenshots went viral for
all the wrong reasons **[CHECK: Unity launch, Nov 2014]**.
`[VISUAL: a cartoon character with only eyes and teeth floating in an empty head; the internet laughing]`

Ubisoft apologized. And here's the dangerous part: **both games still sold millions**
**[CHECK: Watch Dogs ~10M, Unity sales]**.
`[VISUAL: a cash register ringing over the faceless character]`

When you ship a broken game and still make your money, you learn the wrong lesson.

### 5:30 The Belief

Somewhere along the way, Ubisoft stopped asking what players wanted, and started believing
one thing. Every decision after this point traces back to that one sentence:

**"If we make it bigger, they'll keep buying."**
`[VISUAL: the sentence types out on a dark screen, one word at a time]`

Bigger maps. Bigger budgets. More games running for years with more things to buy inside
them. For a while, the numbers backed it up. Then Ubisoft spent the next ten years proving
that bigger isn't the same as better.

And the culture inside the company was cracking too. In the summer of 2020, dozens of
employees went public with allegations of harassment and misconduct inside Ubisoft. Several
senior executives left, including the company's chief creative officer, the man who
had shaped what a Ubisoft game felt like for years **[CHECK: July 2020 departures, incl. Serge Hascoët]**.
`[VISUAL: an office tower at night; one by one, nameplates are removed from doors]`

The person most responsible for the creative direction was gone. The formula stayed.

### 6:40 Pirates for a Decade

Start with the pirates.

Around 2013, Ubisoft starts work on a pirate game, built on the naval combat fans loved in
*Assassin's Creed IV: Black Flag* **[CHECK: development start ~2013]**.
`[VISUAL: a pirate ship sailing out of a fog bank]`

It gets delayed. Then delayed again. And again. Reports say the budget passed
**two hundred million dollars** **[CHECK: Skull and Bones budget]**. Developers inside
reportedly joked that the project was "too big to fail" **[CHECK]**.
`[VISUAL: a calendar flipping year after year, 2013 to 2024, while the ship is still unfinished in a dry dock]`

When *Skull and Bones* finally launches in February 2024, Ubisoft's CEO calls it a
"**quadruple-A** game". That's a label no one in the industry had ever used before
**[CHECK: Guillemot "AAAA" quote]**.
`[VISUAL: a glowing "AAAA" badge pinned to the ship's sail]`

Players called it something else: boring. Reviews were cold, the player count sank, and
eleven years of work quietly faded **[CHECK: review scores / player numbers]**.
`[VISUAL: the ship anchored alone in a flat, empty grey sea]`

But before that ship even sailed, Ubisoft tried something stranger.

### 8:00 Selling Pixels

December 2021. Ubisoft launches **Quartz**, a platform selling NFTs, which were unique
digital items on a blockchain, inside *Ghost Recon Breakpoint*. A helmet. A gun. Each with
a serial number **[CHECK: Quartz launch Dec 2021]**.
`[VISUAL: a soldier's helmet spinning on a pedestal with a glowing serial number]`

The trailer for it got so many dislikes that Ubisoft unlisted it. Reports said only a
handful of the items were actually claimed. A few months later, Ubisoft quietly stopped
releasing new ones **[CHECK: Quartz trailer dislikes, items claimed, wind-down 2022]**.
`[VISUAL: a dislike counter spinning up; the pedestal slowly sinking into the floor]`

Players didn't want to own a numbered helmet. They wanted good games. But Ubisoft kept
asking how to make more money from the same players, not how to give them a reason to stay.

But one failure, or two, couldn't slow Ubisoft down. Because the next game had the biggest
brand in movie history behind it.

### 9:00 The Bad Guess

August 2024. *Star Wars Outlaws*. The first open-world Star Wars game ever made.
`[VISUAL: a lone smuggler on a desert planet; twin suns setting]`

This was supposed to be the easy win. Star Wars, the Ubisoft formula and a launch on every
platform.

It sold about **one million copies in its first month**. Ubisoft admitted the sales were
"**softer than expected**" and lowered its targets for the whole year.
`[VISUAL: a "1,000,000" counter that slows, sputters and stops short of a goal line]`

And here's the part fans haven't forgotten. When the CEO was asked what went wrong, his
explanation was that the Star Wars brand was in "**choppy waters**". In other words, people
were bored of Star Wars.
`[VISUAL: a lightsaber flickering out]`

Not the formula. Not the bugs. Not the price. The brand.

At the same moment, Ubisoft delayed its next *Assassin's Creed*, the game meant to rescue
the year. The CEO said it was delayed so it wouldn't repeat Outlaws' "less than perfect"
launch.
`[VISUAL: an assassin frozen mid-leap as a big "DELAYED" banner unrolls]`

The company's board reportedly opened an internal investigation into the back-to-back
failures **[CHECK: board investigation]**.

Meanwhile, Ubisoft had already placed another bet: a free-to-play shooter called
**XDefiant**, built to compete with *Call of Duty*. It launched in 2024 to a strong start.
Within months, players drifted away. Ubisoft announced it would shut the game down, and
closed studios in San Francisco and Osaka along with it **[CHECK: XDefiant launch May 2024,
shutdown announced Dec 2024, servers off June 2025, ~277 jobs]**.
`[VISUAL: a busy multiplayer lobby emptying, player names greying out one by one]`

A game built to run for years was gone in about one.

And the game fans actually begged for? In 2020, Ubisoft announced a remake of *Prince of
Persia: The Sands of Time*. The trailer was mocked for looking like an old phone game. It was
delayed, delayed again, and then moved to a different studio to be rebuilt from scratch
**[CHECK: remake announced Sept 2020; reassigned to Ubisoft Montreal 2022; current status]**.
`[VISUAL: an hourglass whose sand runs out, flips itself, and runs out again]`

The one thing players asked for kept slipping away, while the things nobody asked for kept
shipping. And then Ubisoft did something that made players angrier than any bad game.

### 10:40 The Game You Bought and Lost

*The Crew* was a racing game you paid full price for. In 2024, Ubisoft switched off its
servers, and because the game needed those servers to run, the game simply stopped working.
Then Ubisoft revoked the licenses, so the game disappeared from players' libraries
**[CHECK: The Crew shutdown date + license removal details]**.
`[VISUAL: a car on a highway pixelates and dissolves; a "LICENSE REVOKED" stamp lands on the box]`

People hadn't rented that game. They'd bought it. And now it was gone.

Around the same time, Ubisoft's head of subscriptions said the quiet part out loud. Gamers,
he said, need to get "**comfortable with not owning**" their games, the same way people got
used to not owning movies on Netflix **[CHECK: Philippe Tremblay quote, 2024]**.
`[VISUAL: a game box turning transparent, then into a monthly invoice]`

Shutting down *The Crew* started a movement called **Stop Killing Games**. It became a European
citizens' petition that passed **one million signatures**, demanding that companies stop
deleting games people paid for **[CHECK: ECI signature count + date]**.
`[VISUAL: a petition counter racing past one million; a crowd of player silhouettes behind it]`

To players, the message was clear. Ubisoft didn't think of you as a player. It thought of
you as a subscriber.

### 11:50 Locked Out of Their Own Game

Then came the hack.

Around the end of 2025, someone got into the systems behind *Rainbow Six Siege*, Ubisoft's
biggest online game. Suddenly, players logged in to find **billions of credits** in their
accounts. One estimate put the giveaway at about **thirteen million dollars** of real-world
value.
`[VISUAL: a coin counter in a game menu spinning into the billions; credits raining down]`

Ubisoft had to shut down the game and its marketplace. Then it happened again: players
reported random **sixty-seven-day bans** and fresh outages in January 2026.
`[VISUAL: a "SERVERS OFFLINE" screen; a ban notice with "67 days" in red]`

Ubisoft rolled back purchases and handed out login rewards as an apology. For weeks, the
company that wanted you to live inside its games couldn't keep its own front door locked.
`[VISUAL: a castle gate swinging open in the wind, an empty throne room behind it]`

### 12:50 Let's Be Fair

At this point, let's be fair to Ubisoft. Was all of this self-inflicted?

Not entirely. The whole games industry has been in a brutal cycle: tens of thousands of
layoffs across almost every major studio, and blockbuster games now costing hundreds of
millions to make **[CHECK: industry layoff figures]**. Ubisoft is not the only company
cancelling projects.
`[VISUAL: a skyline of game-studio buildings with lights going out one window at a time]`

And *Assassin's Creed Shadows* did eventually launch in 2025, and by many accounts it did
well **[CHECK: Shadows sales/player numbers]**. Ubisoft still has hits.
`[VISUAL: a samurai and a shinobi on a castle wall at dawn]`

But here's the thing. Its competitors went through the same storm, and most of them didn't
lose more than ninety-five percent of their value **[CHECK: % drop from 2018 peak]**.
Ubisoft's problem wasn't the weather. It was the ship.

### 13:50 The Reset

November 2025. Hours before it was due to publish its half-year results, Ubisoft postponed
them and asked the Paris stock exchange to **suspend trading in its own shares**. For about
a week, nobody could buy or sell Ubisoft stock.
`[VISUAL: a stock ticker board where UBISOFT flips to "SUSPENDED"]`

When trading came back, the Tencent deal was finalized: **€1.16 billion** for a minority
stake in a new subsidiary, **Vantage Studios**, home of Assassin's Creed, Far Cry and Rainbow
Six. The family kept control. Again.
`[VISUAL: the three franchise logos being carried into a new building labeled VANTAGE]`

Then came what Ubisoft called a **reset**. It **cancelled six games**. It **closed studios**.
It set a target of cutting about **two hundred million euros** in costs over two years.
Reports put the job cuts at around **twelve hundred people** over the year
**[CHECK: names of cancelled games / closed studios; layoff total]**.
`[VISUAL: six game covers on a wall, each covered by a "CANCELLED" stamp]`

And in the same announcement, Ubisoft said it would lean harder on **artificial intelligence**
to make games **[CHECK: details of AI pivot in the restructuring announcement]**. For a company
whose fans already felt its games were made on an assembly line, that landed about as well
as you'd expect.
`[VISUAL: the assembly line from earlier, now run by robot arms]`

For the full year, Ubisoft reported a **record operating loss of around one point three
billion euros** **[CHECK: FY2026 figure]**. Its market value fell below a billion euros for
the first time since 2012. Today, it's around **six hundred and eighty million**.
`[VISUAL: the stock chart's red line slides off the bottom of the screen]`

Think about where that leaves the family. They spent three years and a fortune fighting off
Vivendi to keep control of Ubisoft. They kept control. But the company they kept is now worth
a fraction of what Vivendi would have paid for it.
`[VISUAL: the five silhouettes on the peak from earlier, now standing at the bottom of the red line]`

The company that once fought off a billionaire's takeover is now worth less than the price
someone paid for a minority stake in three of its games.

### 15:20 What Ubisoft Forgot

So, is Ubisoft finally getting what it deserves?

In my opinion, yes. Not because its developers stopped being talented. They're the reason
any of this was ever good. And not because of one bad year.
`[VISUAL: a quiet studio at night, a single monitor glowing with concept art]`

Ubisoft got here because it believed that **bigger** would always beat **better**. Bigger
maps over better worlds. Bigger budgets over better ideas. Bigger ways to keep you paying
over better reasons to keep you playing.
`[VISUAL: the icon-covered map from earlier; the icons start blinking out one by one]`

The brothers from Brittany built Ubisoft on worlds people wanted to live in. Somewhere along
the way, the company started building worlds to fill instead.
`[VISUAL: back to the farmhouse kitchen table, now empty, one floppy disk left on it]`

In 2018, a company worth around **twelve billion euros** that players loved **[CHECK: peak market cap ≈ €107 × ~113M shares]**.
In 2026, a company worth six hundred and eighty million, with a formula, a Tencent check and a hacker's bill.
Whether there's a third chapter depends on one thing: whether Ubisoft remembers what it was
before it was big.
`[VISUAL: the cracked Ubisoft logo; one crack slowly glows gold]`

Tell me in the comments: what's the last Ubisoft game you actually finished?

And if you like watching companies get what's coming, subscribe. Brand Receipts does this
every week.
`[VISUAL: the end screen with the Nintendo and PlayStation videos]`
