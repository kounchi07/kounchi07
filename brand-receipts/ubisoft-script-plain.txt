Ubisoft Is (Finally) Getting What It Deserves…

In 2025, the Chinese giant Tencent paid one point one six billion euros for a minority stake in a new company that holds just three of Ubisoft's series: Assassin's Creed, Far Cry and Rainbow Six.

Today, the entire company, every studio and every game, is worth about six hundred and eighty million euros.

Back in 2018, one Ubisoft share cost over a hundred euros. This week it closed around four.

Six games cancelled. Studios closed. A record loss. And a hacker who handed out millions of dollars' worth of free currency inside their biggest online game.

So how does the company that made Assassin's Creed end up worth less than what someone paid for a slice of just three of its games? To answer that, you have to see how high Ubisoft climbed. The way it climbed is exactly how it fell.

1986. A small town in Brittany, in the northwest of France. Five brothers named Guillemot, whose family sold farm supplies, start a business selling software by mail order.

They don't stay a distributor for long. In 1995 they release Rayman, a cartoon hero with no arms and no legs, and it sells millions.

Then they start collecting worlds. They buy Red Storm, the studio behind the Tom Clancy games, and suddenly Ubisoft owns Rainbow Six, Ghost Recon and, in 2002, Splinter Cell. In 2003 comes Prince of Persia: The Sands of Time, a game people still call a masterpiece.

And in 2007, a studio in Montreal releases a game about a hooded assassin climbing real historical cities. Assassin's Creed becomes one of the biggest new series of its generation.

Then Ubisoft finds a second engine. Far Cry turns into a blockbuster series. Just Dance, a game where you just copy the moves on screen, sells to families who have never bought a "real" video game. Ubisoft's studios spread across the world: Montreal, Toronto, Shanghai, Kyiv, Pune. Tens of thousands of people build Ubisoft games.

Ubisoft is now a giant. And then the giant gets hunted.

In 2015, the French billionaire Vincent Bolloré, through his media group Vivendi, starts quietly buying Ubisoft shares. The brothers recognize the playbook: Vivendi had just taken over another game company, Gameloft, the same way.

Vivendi keeps buying, until it owns more than a quarter of Ubisoft. The brothers fly around the world asking investors to stand with them. They open studios, announce games and push the share price up, because the higher the price, the more expensive a takeover becomes.

The Guillemots fight for three years. In 2018 they win. Vivendi sells, Tencent buys a small stake as a friendly shield, and the family keeps control. That same year, Ubisoft's share price hits its all-time high.

They had beaten a billionaire. They had built worlds millions of people loved. And they had found something even more valuable: a formula.

Here's how it worked. Build a huge open world. Cover the map in icons: towers to climb, outposts to capture, collectibles to find. Climb a tower and the map reveals more icons.

It worked in Assassin's Creed. It worked in Far Cry. It worked in Watch Dogs. Players even gave it a name: the Ubisoft formula.

And for a company that has to ship games on schedule to keep shareholders happy, a formula is a dream. You don't have to invent something new every time. You build a bigger map and add more icons.

But a formula only works as long as the people buying it haven't noticed it's a formula.

The first cracks showed up in 2014.

At a trade show in 2012, Ubisoft had shown Watch Dogs: rain-soaked streets, realistic lighting, a game that looked a generation ahead. When it launched in 2014, it looked noticeably worse, and players accused Ubisoft of a "downgrade".

A few months later came Assassin's Creed Unity. It launched so broken that characters walked around with no faces, just floating eyes and teeth. The screenshots went viral for all the wrong reasons.

Ubisoft apologized. And here's the dangerous part: both games still sold millions.

When you ship a broken game and still make your money, you learn the wrong lesson.

Somewhere along the way, Ubisoft stopped asking what players wanted, and started believing one thing. Every decision after this point traces back to that one sentence:

"If we make it bigger, they'll keep buying."

Bigger maps. Bigger budgets. More games running for years with more things to buy inside them. For a while, the numbers backed it up. Then Ubisoft spent the next ten years proving that bigger isn't the same as better.

And the culture inside the company was cracking too. In the summer of 2020, dozens of employees went public with allegations of harassment and misconduct inside Ubisoft. Several senior executives left, including the company's chief creative officer, the man who had shaped what a Ubisoft game felt like for years.

The person most responsible for the creative direction was gone. The formula stayed.

Start with the pirates.

Around 2013, Ubisoft starts work on a pirate game, built on the naval combat fans loved in Assassin's Creed IV: Black Flag.

It gets delayed. Then delayed again. And again. Reports say the budget passed two hundred million dollars. Developers inside reportedly joked that the project was "too big to fail".

When Skull and Bones finally launches in February 2024, Ubisoft's CEO calls it a "quadruple-A game". That's a label no one in the industry had ever used before.

Players called it something else: boring. Reviews were cold, the player count sank, and eleven years of work quietly faded.

But before that ship even sailed, Ubisoft tried something stranger.

December 2021. Ubisoft launches Quartz, a platform selling NFTs, which were unique digital items on a blockchain, inside Ghost Recon Breakpoint. A helmet. A gun. Each with a serial number.

The trailer for it got so many dislikes that Ubisoft unlisted it. Reports said only a handful of the items were actually claimed. A few months later, Ubisoft quietly stopped releasing new ones.

Players didn't want to own a numbered helmet. They wanted good games. But Ubisoft kept asking how to make more money from the same players, not how to give them a reason to stay.

But one failure, or two, couldn't slow Ubisoft down. Because the next game had the biggest brand in movie history behind it.

August 2024. Star Wars Outlaws. The first open-world Star Wars game ever made.

This was supposed to be the easy win. Star Wars, the Ubisoft formula and a launch on every platform.

It sold about one million copies in its first month. Ubisoft admitted the sales were "softer than expected" and lowered its targets for the whole year.

And here's the part fans haven't forgotten. When the CEO was asked what went wrong, his explanation was that the Star Wars brand was in "choppy waters". In other words, people were bored of Star Wars.

Not the formula. Not the bugs. Not the price. The brand.

At the same moment, Ubisoft delayed its next Assassin's Creed, the game meant to rescue the year. The CEO said it was delayed so it wouldn't repeat Outlaws' "less than perfect" launch.

The company's board reportedly opened an internal investigation into the back-to-back failures.

Meanwhile, Ubisoft had already placed another bet: a free-to-play shooter called XDefiant, built to compete with Call of Duty. It launched in 2024 to a strong start. Within months, players drifted away. Ubisoft announced it would shut the game down, and closed studios in San Francisco and Osaka along with it.

A game built to run for years was gone in about one.

And the game fans actually begged for? In 2020, Ubisoft announced a remake of Prince of Persia: The Sands of Time. The trailer was mocked for looking like an old phone game. It was delayed, delayed again, and then moved to a different studio to be rebuilt from scratch.

The one thing players asked for kept slipping away, while the things nobody asked for kept shipping. And then Ubisoft did something that made players angrier than any bad game.

The Crew was a racing game you paid full price for. In 2024, Ubisoft switched off its servers, and because the game needed those servers to run, the game simply stopped working. Then Ubisoft revoked the licenses, so the game disappeared from players' libraries.

People hadn't rented that game. They'd bought it. And now it was gone.

Around the same time, Ubisoft's head of subscriptions said the quiet part out loud. Gamers, he said, need to get "comfortable with not owning" their games, the same way people got used to not owning movies on Netflix.

Shutting down The Crew started a movement called Stop Killing Games. It became a European citizens' petition that passed one million signatures, demanding that companies stop deleting games people paid for.

To players, the message was clear. Ubisoft didn't think of you as a player. It thought of you as a subscriber.

Then came the hack.

Around the end of 2025, someone got into the systems behind Rainbow Six Siege, Ubisoft's biggest online game. Suddenly, players logged in to find billions of credits in their accounts. One estimate put the giveaway at about thirteen million dollars of real-world value.

Ubisoft had to shut down the game and its marketplace. Then it happened again: players reported random sixty-seven-day bans and fresh outages in January 2026.

Ubisoft rolled back purchases and handed out login rewards as an apology. For weeks, the company that wanted you to live inside its games couldn't keep its own front door locked.

At this point, let's be fair to Ubisoft. Was all of this self-inflicted?

Not entirely. The whole games industry has been in a brutal cycle: tens of thousands of layoffs across almost every major studio, and blockbuster games now costing hundreds of millions to make. Ubisoft is not the only company cancelling projects.

And Assassin's Creed Shadows did eventually launch in 2025, and by many accounts it did well. Ubisoft still has hits.

But here's the thing. Its competitors went through the same storm, and most of them didn't lose more than ninety-five percent of their value. Ubisoft's problem wasn't the weather. It was the ship.

November 2025. Hours before it was due to publish its half-year results, Ubisoft postponed them and asked the Paris stock exchange to suspend trading in its own shares. For about a week, nobody could buy or sell Ubisoft stock.

When trading came back, the Tencent deal was finalized: €1.16 billion for a minority stake in a new subsidiary, Vantage Studios, home of Assassin's Creed, Far Cry and Rainbow Six. The family kept control. Again.

Then came what Ubisoft called a reset. It cancelled six games. It closed studios. It set a target of cutting about two hundred million euros in costs over two years. Reports put the job cuts at around twelve hundred people over the year.

And in the same announcement, Ubisoft said it would lean harder on artificial intelligence to make games. For a company whose fans already felt its games were made on an assembly line, that landed about as well as you'd expect.

For the full year, Ubisoft reported a record operating loss of around one point three billion euros. Its market value fell below a billion euros for the first time since 2012. Today, it's around six hundred and eighty million.

Think about where that leaves the family. They spent three years and a fortune fighting off Vivendi to keep control of Ubisoft. They kept control. But the company they kept is now worth a fraction of what Vivendi would have paid for it.

The company that once fought off a billionaire's takeover is now worth less than the price someone paid for a minority stake in three of its games.

So, is Ubisoft finally getting what it deserves?

In my opinion, yes. Not because its developers stopped being talented. They're the reason any of this was ever good. And not because of one bad year.

Ubisoft got here because it believed that bigger would always beat better. Bigger maps over better worlds. Bigger budgets over better ideas. Bigger ways to keep you paying over better reasons to keep you playing.

The brothers from Brittany built Ubisoft on worlds people wanted to live in. Somewhere along the way, the company started building worlds to fill instead.

In 2018, a company worth around twelve billion euros that players loved. In 2026, a company worth six hundred and eighty million, with a formula, a Tencent check and a hacker's bill. Whether there's a third chapter depends on one thing: whether Ubisoft remembers what it was before it was big.
